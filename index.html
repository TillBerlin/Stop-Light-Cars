Results

What the simulation shows

Nine findings, each tested across ten scenarios rather than one. In every chart the dark line is the rush-hour reference and the faint lines are the other nine scenarios, so you can see immediately whether a result holds generally or only at one operating point.

The ten scenarios

What was tested, and against what

Every finding was run against all ten streets below. Each varies one thing from the reference, so a difference in the result can be attributed to that one thing. Nine are saturated — more cars arrive than the lane can discharge — because that is the only regime in which a capacity difference can appear at all. The tenth is quiet on purpose, as a control.

Why the first one is the reference. It is the most ordinary street of the ten, not the most favourable: a busy but not extreme arrival rate, a signal cycle of a common length, the 50 km/h limit typical of a larger Dutch through-road, only seven drivers in ten taking part, and a population containing cautious, normal and assertive people rather than one temperament. It is deliberately not the best case — three other scenarios score higher. It is also the state this page opens in, so anything quoted without qualification can be reproduced by pressing Play.

  1. Lane B moves about 30% more cars

    +28.6%41.4 cars in Lane A against 53.3 in Lane B · waiting time falls from 98s to 75s

    At realistic rush-hour settings, with only 70% of drivers taking part and a mixed population, the larger resting gap moves roughly thirty percent more traffic through the same signal. Across the nine saturated scenarios the gain ranges from +15.4%, where only three drivers in ten comply, to +33.9% in a population that alternates between cautious and aggressive. The eight findings that follow describe what moves that number.

  2. Compliance pays back steadily, with no threshold to clear

    +15.4% at 30% compliancerising smoothly to +28.6% at 70% and +35.0% if everyone takes part

    The gain grows in proportion to the share of drivers who adopt the larger gap, at roughly four to five points of advantage for every ten points of compliance. There is no minimum participation below which the idea fails and no point at which it saturates, and the curve is monotonic in all ten scenarios — the most robust result on this page. That matters for whether the idea is worth trying at all: a scheme that only paid off once nearly everyone complied would be far harder to justify than one that returns something from the first participants onward.

  3. Nothing happens until the lane is saturated

    1.00 below 6 cars/min → +28.7% at 12a lane discharges roughly 8–9 cars per minute; below that both lanes clear everything

    This is the least surprising finding here and the most important to state, because it bounds every other one. Below capacity there is nothing to win: both lanes clear every car that arrives, the ratio sits at exactly 1.00, and no amount of compliance or paint changes it. The advantage appears as demand crosses what a lane can discharge, around 8 to 10 cars per minute, and reaches full size by about 12. The quiet scenario in these charts is flat at 1.00 for exactly this reason — it is a control demonstrating the boundary, not a failure of the idea. Any real-world trial that measured an off-peak street would correctly find no effect.

  4. The striped zone needs about 40 metres

    +1.6% at 10m → +24.0% at 30m → +28.9% at 40mflat from 40m onward at the reference settings

    A short zone achieves nothing, because it does not reach back far enough to hold the cars whose delay matters. The curve climbs steeply between 20 and 40 metres and then stops. At a six-metre intended distance, 40 metres is roughly the first four cars in the queue, which supports the natural reading: the gain comes from releasing the front of the queue quickly, and everyone behind inherits the delay those cars avoid. There is one clear exception in the faint lines — with a long 45-second green the curve keeps climbing past 40 metres, because a long phase discharges more of the queue and so more of the queue is worth spacing out.

  5. A long green needs a longer striped zone, not a shorter one

    +30.6% at 15s → +22.1% at 50sbut lengthening the zone to 100m recovers about half of that loss, to +26.3%

    The advantage falls away as green phases lengthen, and the obvious explanations turn out to be wrong. It is not a measurement artifact of few cycles fitting into a short run: repeating every point with runs six times longer leaves the decline intact. It is not a loss of saturation as capacity rises with green time either — tripling demand to 30 cars per minute changes nothing at all.

    What does explain about half of it is that the striped zone becomes too short. A long green discharges more of the queue, so more of the queue is worth spacing out, and a 50-metre zone stops covering the cars that matter. Extending it to 100 metres lifts a 50-second phase from +22.1% to +26.3% while changing nothing at a 20-second phase, exactly as that explanation predicts. The remainder is the genuine effect: given enough green time Lane A eventually clears its queue too, and an advantage in starting matters less the more time everyone has to finish. The practical reading is that the striped zone should be scaled to the signal, not fixed.

  6. Higher speed limits help, up to about 60 km/h

    +18.1% at 20 km/h → +31.2% at 60 km/hno further change between 60 and 100 km/h

    On a slow street the advantage is roughly half what it is on a faster one. A simultaneous start only becomes throughput if cars can then accelerate away and leave the junction; at 20 km/h they cannot use the room they were given, so much of the benefit is stranded. Above 60 km/h nothing more is gained, because the limiting factor becomes how quickly drivers get moving rather than how fast they may eventually travel. At 50 km/h — a normal limit on larger Dutch through-roads — the reference scenario sits just below the plateau.

  7. Only uniformly aggressive traffic gains noticeably less

    +32.1% very cautious → +19.8% aggressiveroughly flat across the first four levels, then a drop at the top

    Sweeping the whole population from very cautious to aggressive leaves the advantage broadly unchanged through the first four levels, with no consistent direction. Only a uniformly aggressive population is clearly worse, and it is the lowest point in eight of the ten scenarios. That fits the mechanism: aggressive drivers already start quickly and follow closely, so there is less start-up delay left for the larger gap to recover. Meanwhile the absolute numbers change enormously — an all-cautious Lane A moves 28 cars where an all-aggressive one moves 63 — so temperament dominates how much traffic a junction carries while barely affecting how much the idea adds on top.

  8. Dissimilar drivers make the idea more useful, not less

    +26.9% identical drivers → +33.9% alternating extremesthe opposite of what we expected

    We expected a population of similar drivers to coordinate better, since a simultaneous start seems to need people who move alike. The data says the reverse. Widening the spread of personalities leaves the advantage flat or slightly higher in the reference scenario, and the strongest single result on this page comes from the deliberately worst-behaved population: drivers alternating between very cautious and aggressive, at +33.9%, against +26.9% when every driver is identical.

    The reason is visible in the absolute numbers. Alternating traffic hurts both lanes, but it hurts Lane A far more: Lane A falls from 42.0 cars to 36.1 while Lane B only falls from 53.3 to 48.4. In a tightly packed lane one slow starter blocks everyone behind, whereas the larger gap gives following drivers room to start on their own account. The spacing acts as insulation against an unpredictable queue — which would make the idea more attractive on real streets, where drivers are never uniform. This is also the one finding where the scenarios genuinely disagree: with a short 12-second green the effect reverses. Treat the direction as unsettled and the original hypothesis as unsupported rather than reversed.

    This finding is the most exposed to the aborted-start problem described in the limitations. A mixed population is precisely where a driver most often pulls away and then has to stop again, because the car in front turned out to be a slow starter. Every such event costs a full second start-up that this model may not charge in full. Since the effect would fall hardest on the lane with the larger gaps, where drivers are likeliest to set off on their own account, it could reduce or even reverse the advantage reported here.

  9. Four and a half metres is already enough

    +25.0% at 4m → +28.6% at 4.5mflat from there to 8m, which reaches only +30.8%

    Only the smallest gap tested is measurably worse. From about four and a half metres the curve is level, and the six-metre default sits comfortably inside the plateau rather than at its edge. The explanation the numbers support is that the gap has exactly one job — letting several drivers pull away at the same moment — and once that is achieved the queue settles into an ordinary slow-moving line at its normal following distance, absorbing whatever spacing it started from. This is convenient for street design: the marking needs to be big enough rather than precisely calibrated, and nothing is wasted by overshooting.

    Treat this as the least trustworthy result on the page. It depends entirely on how faithfully the model handles a driver who pulls away into a gap and then has to stop again because the car in front has not moved. A smaller gap makes that happen more often, and a real driver who stops and restarts pays the whole start-up delay a second time, losing the benefit completely. If the model under-represents that cost — and there is reason to think it does — then small gaps look better here than they would on a real street, and the plateau would begin later than 4.5 metres. The six-metre rule is the safer choice regardless.

Two caveats above all others. First, each chart varies a single parameter around a fixed scenario, and the parameters interact. Ten scenarios is far more than one, but the full space is larger still, and this page has already been wrong once in a way these sweeps would not have caught: an earlier version reported that driver temperament barely mattered, which was an artifact of a model in which every driver in a population was numerically identical. Where the faint lines agree, a finding is probably general; where they disagree, treat the direction as unsettled.

Second, and more seriously, the model may not charge enough for a start that has to be abandoned. A driver who pulls away and then stops again because the car in front stayed put loses the entire benefit, and on a real street pays the start-up delay twice over. That failure mode becomes more common as gaps get smaller and as drivers become less alike, which is precisely where two of the findings above are least certain — the claim that 4.5 metres is already enough, and the claim about dissimilar drivers. Both are flagged in place. None of this is a prediction about real streets; it is an argument that a careful real-world trial, one that counts aborted starts as well as cars, would be worth running.

The idea

More space can mean a quicker start

Much of the waiting at a green light happens because drivers start one after another. Each driver waits for the car ahead to move, then reacts and starts.

1 → 2 → 3Small gaps

Cars leave in a chain, passing the start-up delay down the queue.

1 + 2 + 3Larger gaps

Several cars have room to begin moving at the same time.

How the model works

How the cars behave

This is a simple car-following model. Each car watches the light, the car ahead and the space between them.

01

Joining the queue

Cars slow down for a red light and stop behind the car ahead. Lane A uses a normal two-metre gap. In the striped zone, compliant Lane B drivers aim for the selected intended distance (4-8 metres, default six).

02

Starting on green

The first car reacts to the green light. Other drivers start after the car ahead moves or when there is already enough clear space. A larger gap can therefore let several drivers react together.

03

Following safely

Moving cars adjust their speed to keep a safe distance. They accelerate smoothly, brake as the gap closes and use stronger braking if a collision becomes likely.

04

Signals and new cars

Drivers stop for red and decide whether it is safe to stop on orange. New cars arrive at random intervals averaging the chosen rate, and wait off-screen if the road entrance is full.

Key terms

The four numbers that describe a driver

Start-up time seconds
How long a stopped driver takes to get going once it is their turn. The countdown begins when the light is green and the car ahead has started moving, or when there is already enough clear space. The car stays still until it finishes. This is the delay that passes down a queue one car at a time, and the delay the larger gap is meant to avoid.
Headway seconds
The time gap a moving driver wants to keep to the car ahead — roughly "how many seconds behind". It is a time, not a distance, so the gap grows with speed: at 50 km/h a 1.5-second headway is about 21 metres. It shrinks to nothing at a standstill, where the resting gap takes over instead.
Jerk m/s³
How fast the acceleration itself is allowed to change — in plain terms, how briskly a driver moves the pedal. Low jerk is a smooth, gradual squeeze; high jerk is a quick jab. It does not change how hard a car can eventually accelerate or brake, only how quickly it gets there, which is what makes a start feel gentle or sharp.
Acceleration and braking m/s²
The strongest pull-away and the firmest comfortable stop a driver will normally choose. These are everyday limits, not the limits of the car. When a collision looks likely the driver abandons them and brakes as hard as the situation requires.

Driver profiles

What aggressiveness actually changes

"Aggressiveness" is not a single dial inside the model. It is a bundle of the four values above, moved together: a more aggressive driver starts sooner, follows closer, works the pedals harder and accepts firmer braking. Crucially it is a continuous quantity rather than five categories. The five named levels below are anchors on that scale, and a driver can sit anywhere between them. The Driver mix slider sets the range of personalities present; each car draws its own point in that range when it appears and keeps that character for its whole run.

Values between the anchors are interpolated, so a driver two-thirds of the way from cautious to normal gets two-thirds of each difference. On top of that, every trait is given its own small random nudge — about 5% — once, when the car is created. The result is that no two drivers in a queue are numerically identical, even when the mix is set to a single level, while a cautious driver still cannot accidentally out-accelerate an aggressive one. Emergency braking is deliberately not a personality setting: once a collision is predicted, every driver builds brake pressure at the same 10 m/s³ vehicle limit, because that is a property of the car rather than the person.

Gaps at a standstill

Where the idea lives

Headway describes moving traffic and fades to zero at rest, so a separate resting gap governs a stopped queue. Lane A always uses 2 metres. In the striped zone, compliant Lane B drivers instead target the Lane B intended distance (4–8 metres, 6 by default) — the whole point of the experiment. A driver commits to that larger gap while still braking, as soon as it can predict its place in a queue forming on red, and holds it while it creeps and waits. The moment the light turns green the queue is dissolving rather than forming, so even a car still standing reverts to the ordinary 2-metre gap. Outside the striped zone, and for non-compliant drivers, Lane B behaves exactly like Lane A.

The simulation itself

How the numbers are produced

The model advances in 50-millisecond steps, recalculating arrivals, the signal, queueing, driver behaviour and car physics on every step. Vehicles are 3.8 to 5.2 metres long, drawn when each car is created, so a queue of ten cars is not a fixed length. The batch statistics are not a faster approximation: every point on the graph runs this same timestep with seeded randomness, which is why results are reproducible but a sweep takes a moment to compute.

New cars arrive as a random process, not a metronome. The arrival rate sets how many cars are expected each minute; the actual gaps between them vary, so a stream at 15 cars per minute sometimes delivers two cars nose to tail and sometimes leaves a long hole. A fixed interval was not only unrealistic, it could fall into step with the signal cycle and make throughput look steadier than it is. Both lanes always draw from the same arrival stream, so they face identical demand and any difference between them comes from the resting-gap rule rather than from luck.

Limitations

What we still need to learn

The simulation shows a possibility, not a prediction. The idea needs safe real-world trials and independent evaluation before it could guide street design.

  • Real drivers may behave differently. A model cannot capture every reaction, mistake or local driving habit.
  • Starting and having to stop again is probably under-costed. A driver who pulls away into the gap, finds the car in front has not moved, and must stop again loses the whole benefit — and in practice pays the start-up delay twice. This model does let a driver abort a start, but it may not charge enough for it, and it does not reproduce the human experience of creeping forward, stopping, and beginning again. The effect would fall hardest exactly where the larger gap is meant to help, because that is where a driver is most likely to set off on their own judgement rather than follow the car ahead. Anyone trialling this should measure aborted starts directly.
  • Participation is uncertain. Independent research should compare different ways to encourage the larger gap. Messages may need to fit the local language and culture, although drivers may also learn from one another once the benefit becomes visible.
  • Not every junction has enough room. The queue needs sufficient road between this light and the previous junction. Larger gaps could otherwise block side streets, crossings or access points.
  • Turning traffic can disrupt the pattern. Cars waiting to turn left or right, or stopping inside the junction, may prevent a coordinated start.
  • The model simplifies the street. It does not represent lane changes, buses, bicycles, pedestrians, emergency vehicles, hills, poor weather or different vehicle performance.
  • Safety must come first. A simultaneous start depends on good visibility and predictable behaviour. Trials must check for new risks to people crossing and for unexpected braking.
  • Results depend on local conditions. Traffic volume, signal timing, queue length and the share of compliant drivers may change the benefit or remove it altogether.
  • One light is not the whole journey. The model ends when a car crosses the stop line. It does not include the next junction or a queue further down the road, which could remove the time saved here or shift congestion somewhere else.
